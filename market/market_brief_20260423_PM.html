Market commentary

US equities are retreating Thursday from Wednesday's record closes — the S&P 500 falling to 7,109 (down 0.41%) and the Nasdaq slipping 0.45% — as a cluster of heavyweight earnings from ServiceNow and IBM disappointed investors while oil surges for a fourth consecutive session on the ongoing US-Iran Strait of Hormuz standoff. The sell-off is concentrated in high-growth software (SaaS) stocks, where ServiceNow's subscription revenue miss triggered its worst session in years, dragging peers like Snowflake, Workday, and Salesforce lower in sympathy. Notably, small-cap stocks (Russell 2000) are bucking the trend with a 0.74% gain, suggesting some rotation into domestically-focused companies less exposed to international geopolitical risks — a pattern known as "risk rotation" rather than full-blown risk-off. WTI crude (the US oil benchmark, priced in dollars per barrel) rising to $94.46 and Brent (the global benchmark, more sensitive to Middle East supply) topping $103 signals the market believes the Hormuz disruption is not yet resolved, keeping inflation fears alive and complicating the Federal Reserve's one-cut-in-2026 outlook. Gold at $4,758 continues its safe-haven climb. This compares to Wednesday's euphoria, when the S&P 500 hit an all-time high of 7,137.90 and the Nasdaq reached 24,657 on optimism around the ceasefire extension — today's pullback is a reminder that sentiment can swing quickly when geopolitics and earnings collide.

US-Iran Peace Talks Stall — Strait of Hormuz Remains Choked, Brent Tops $103 for Fourth Day
#1
The United States and Iran failed to convene for a further round of peace negotiations on Wednesday despite President Trump extending the ceasefire indefinitely on April 22. Iran continues to control traffic through the Strait of Hormuz — the narrow waterway through which roughly 20% of the world's oil supply transits — charging vessels tolls of over $1 million per ship and, according to reports, having seized two additional vessels in disputed waters. Brent crude climbed to $103.38 on Thursday (+1.44%) while WTI reached $94.46 (+1.67%), marking a fourth straight session of gains. Trump has escalated the naval posture by ordering the US Navy to shoot any vessel found laying mines in the strait.

Background context: A two-week ceasefire was brokered by Pakistan on April 7. On April 17, Iran briefly declared the strait open to commercial traffic, sending oil prices crashing below $84/barrel — a 10%+ intraday collapse. Since then, talks have broken down and Brent has surged 23% from that trough, more than reversing all the peace-talk gains. The Hormuz crisis is now the dominant macro variable for every sector: it drives oil prices (energy stocks), inflation expectations (Fed policy), airline fuel costs (consumer), and government procurement delays (enterprise tech).

ServiceNow (NOW) Q1 2026: EPS Beats, But Subscription Revenue Miss Triggers Worst Session in Years — Down 12–15%
#2
ServiceNow reported Q1 2026 results that beat both EPS and total revenue analyst estimates, but subscription revenue growth — the single metric the market cares most about for a SaaS company — missed expectations by approximately 75 basis points (0.75 percentage points). Management attributed the shortfall directly to delayed closures of several large on-premises government deals in the Middle East, where clients paused procurement amid the US-Iran conflict. The stock fell 12–15% in extended trading and has extended losses into Thursday's session, marking one of its worst one-day reactions in years. Peers including Snowflake (SNOW), Datadog (DDOG), and CrowdStrike (CRWD) are selling off in sympathy.

"Subscription revenue" is the recurring monthly/annual fee clients pay for cloud software access — it's the engine of SaaS value. Even a small miss signals something is wrong with demand, and the market re-prices the entire forward growth curve instantly. ServiceNow's explanation is event-driven (the Middle East conflict disrupting government IT procurement), but investors must now decide whether this is temporary or a signal that geopolitical instability is a persistent headwind for enterprise software that relies heavily on government clients.

IBM Q1 2026: Earnings Beat, But Flat Guidance and AI-Disruption Fears Send Shares Down 7–10%
#3
IBM reported Q1 2026 results that beat analyst estimates on both earnings per share and revenue — typically a reason to rally. Instead, the stock fell 7–10% after the company held its full-year guidance entirely unchanged, a significant disappointment in an earnings season where investors expect raised outlooks. The market's reaction was amplified by growing concern that Anthropic's enterprise AI tools are actively displacing IBM's traditional consulting and software business — a theme explicitly flagged in analyst commentary following the report. Software names including Adobe, Salesforce, Workday, Snowflake, HubSpot, and Autodesk fell in sympathy, with IBM as the bellwether.

IBM has spent years rebuilding its identity around hybrid cloud (a mix of on-premise servers and public cloud services) and AI, branded as "IBM watsonx." The bull case was always that enterprise clients would pay IBM's premium for trusted, integrated AI — but Anthropic's rapid enterprise penetration and OpenAI's GPT-4o are now offering frontier AI at a fraction of the integration cost. If clients start redirecting AI consulting spend directly to model providers, IBM's revenue growth trajectory may be structurally lower than the market had assumed. This is the "reverse AI boom" trade: chip and infrastructure companies like NVDA benefit, while legacy AI-adjacent vendors like IBM face margin compression.
