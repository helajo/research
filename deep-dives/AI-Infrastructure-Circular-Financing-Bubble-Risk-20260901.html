AI Infrastructure: Circular Financing & Bubble Risk

A cross-sourced look at the deal structures, leverage, and depreciation debates now moving AI infrastructure stock prices
Date: 2026-09-01 Scope: AI Infrastructure Theme Type: Industry / Thematic Briefing

Executive Summary

FACT A documented circular-financing structure links Nvidia, OpenAI, Oracle, Microsoft, and CoreWeave: capital invested by chipmakers and cloud platforms flows back to them as revenue after passing through one or two AI labs. OpenAI's disclosed infrastructure commitments total well over $1 trillion, against $13.07B of actual FY2025 revenue and a current disclosed run-rate above $40B ahead of a reported IPO.

FACT Hyperscaler depreciation schedules for GPUs have lengthened (4→6 years at Microsoft; 5.5 years at Meta) in the same period Nvidia's product refresh cycle has shortened to roughly annual — a mismatch Michael Burry estimates understates depreciation expense by ~$176B from 2026–2028.

FACT Off-balance-sheet lease and SPV-based debt at the five largest US hyperscalers ($969B in undiscounted future lease commitments, of which $662B has not yet commenced) now exceeds their combined on-balance-sheet debt. The IMF flagged AI-related asset risk in July 2026 as among the highest in three decades, with leverage — not valuation alone — cited as the primary concern.

VIEW Whether this constitutes a "bubble" is a forecast, not a fact. Bears (Burry, IMF) point to leverage, opaque financing, and questionable ROI. Bulls point to real profitability, earnings-funded capex, and materially lower valuation multiples than the dot-com peak. This report lays out both sides on their own evidence rather than adjudicating between them.

1. The Circular Deal Structure

FACT The mechanism repeatedly documented in financial press: Nvidia invests in OpenAI → OpenAI buys cloud compute from Oracle → Oracle buys chips from Nvidia → Nvidia holds an equity stake in CoreWeave → CoreWeave supplies AI infrastructure back to OpenAI. Cash leaves a chipmaker's balance sheet labeled "investment" and returns to its income statement labeled "revenue" after circulating through the loop.

Figure: two loops, both closing at Nvidia. Nvidia puts equity into OpenAI (and into CoreWeave); OpenAI pays CoreWeave and Oracle for compute; CoreWeave and Oracle spend that revenue buying Nvidia GPUs. Cash that left Nvidia labelled "investment" returns labelled "revenue." Note that Oracle's own leg is not circular in isolation — Oracle neither invests in nor receives investment from OpenAI (its exposure is borrowing risk, Section 4). The circularity involving Oracle exists only because Nvidia's capital passes through OpenAI and returns to Nvidia as a chip order.

Sizing the Node at the Centre

FACT Because every loop above closes at Nvidia, its scale sets the scale of the whole question. In Q2 FY2027 (quarter ended 2026-07-26) Nvidia reported revenue of $96.2B, up 106% year over year, of which Data Center was $89.0B, up 117%; GAAP gross margin was 75.0%, and guidance for the following quarter was approximately $108B. Full fiscal 2026 revenue was $216B with roughly $97B of free cash flow. Nvidia also discloses that four direct customers each exceed 10% of revenue and together account for 61% of it — without naming them.

VIEW That last disclosure is the single most important limit on any outside analysis of the loop, including this one. It is granular enough to prove the concentration exists but too coarse to trace a specific hyperscaler's spending decision through to a specific dollar of Nvidia revenue. Every attempt to quantify contagion from Microsoft or Oracle through Nvidia to the second tier runs into this wall — a point the stress test in Section 8 makes explicit rather than papering over.

Revenue Run-Rate and the IPO Signal

FACT The $13.07B above is FY2025 actual booked revenue and understates the current trajectory. OpenAI's CFO disclosed an annualized run-rate above $20B at the end of 2025, and as of 2026-08-13 Bloomberg reported the run-rate had topped $40 billion, ahead of an expected IPO. That materially narrows the commitments-versus-revenue gap the bear case leans on, though the gap remains enormous in absolute terms: roughly $1.1–1.4T of commitments against a $40B run-rate.

VIEW The IPO signal matters more than the revenue figure. OpenAI is the least transparent and most central node in the entire structure precisely because, as a private company, it carries no public disclosure obligations — yet Oracle's and CoreWeave's leveraged bets both depend on its ability to pay. A listing would force audited financials onto that counterparty, and would likely resolve more of this report's open questions than anything else described here, in either direction.

2. The Depreciation Debate

VIEW Michael Burry's May 2026 thesis ("Tracepalooza and the Bezzle") is the most specific bear argument in circulation. It rests on three claims: (1) Nvidia's data-center revenue is concentrated among a small number of large customers; (2) hyperscalers may be understating depreciation by extending assumed GPU useful life; (3) hundreds of billions in data-center lease commitments sit off balance sheet until activated.

How the Mechanism Works

VIEW Scaled across the hyperscalers' combined GPU fleets, this is the whole of Burry's argument. It also explains why the dispute cannot be settled today: the "correct" answer depends on how long hardware bought in 2026 actually remains in production — something only observable years from now.

FACT Burry estimates the accounting mismatch understates depreciation expense by approximately $176 billion from 2026 to 2028 across the major hyperscalers. He puts the per-company effect on 2028 earnings at +26.9% for Oracle and +20.8% for Meta — meaning reported earnings could be materially inflated versus economic reality if his useful-life assumption is correct.

FACT Nvidia took the unusual step of sending sell-side analysts a memo rebutting Burry on stock-based compensation and depreciation. As of August 2026 the market has largely moved against his short-term positioning — an outcome that does not itself resolve which depreciation assumption is correct. Burry discloses bearish options positions against Nvidia and Palantir through Scion Asset Management, so he has a direct financial interest in the thesis being widely adopted, and his public framing of the practice is correspondingly forceful.

The Counter-Evidence: What Older GPUs Are Actually Doing

FACT Burry's case rests on an empirical premise — that GPUs become economically unproductive in roughly 2–3 years — and the secondary market currently contradicts it. The A100, launched in 2020 and therefore around five years old, still rents for roughly $1.09–$5.07 per GPU-hour depending on provider, and still sells used at $4,000–$9,000 against $7,000–$15,000 new. Rental rates across several older Nvidia data-center generations have risen during 2026 rather than decayed. Most directly on point: CoreWeave CEO Mike Intrator stated in August 2026 that the company has A100 capacity contracted out to 2029 at full pricing — implying a realised economic life closer to nine years than to three.

VIEW This materially weakens the strongest version of the bear case. The economics behind it are intuitive: frontier training migrates to the newest silicon, but inference and fine-tuning workloads cascade down to older hardware, where established pipelines and better price-per-token give it a durable floor. Obsolescence for the leading edge is not the same as obsolescence for the fleet. Two honest caveats remain: today's rental strength is itself a product of an acute GPU shortage and would soften if supply caught up or demand cooled, and a contract running to 2029 is a commitment, not yet a realised outcome.

VIEW This report's position: the extended schedules look defensible on the evidence currently available. A useful-life assumption is an estimate, disclosed in 10-K footnotes and reviewed by auditors, and the only real test of a good estimate is whether the hardware stays productive — which it is doing well beyond the two-to-three years the bear case assumes. The more accurate reading is not that a shorter life is being obscured, but that the useful life of a GPU across an entire fleet is genuinely longer than the frontier-refresh cycle implies, because the fleet and the frontier are not the same thing.

VIEW The residual risk is one of timing, not intent. These assumptions were set during an acute GPU shortage that flatters every input feeding them. If supply catches up and older-generation rental rates fall sharply, a six-year schedule becomes considerably harder to defend and this conclusion should be revisited — which is why older-GPU pricing is listed among the monitoring signals in Section 8.

3. Off-Balance-Sheet Debt & Private Credit Exposure

  • FACT Technology companies have moved >$120B of data-center spending off-balance-sheet in approximately 18 months.
  • FACT Private credit lending to AI-related companies has grown from near zero to >$200B in a few years, led by Blackstone, Blue Owl Capital, Apollo, PIMCO, and BlackRock.
  • FACT Example structure: Blue Owl and JPMorgan invested ~$13B (~$10B as debt) into an SPV owning Oracle's OpenAI facility in Abilene, Texas.
  • FACT Per Moody's Ratings (Feb 2026), the five largest US hyperscalers (Amazon, Meta, Alphabet, Microsoft, Oracle) held $969B in total undiscounted future data-center lease commitments as of year-end 2025, of which $662B represents leases not yet commenced — equal to 113% of those companies' combined on-balance-sheet debt.
  • FACT Four US senators called for investigation into Big Tech's use of "complex and opaque debt markets," warning of potential "destabilizing losses" for financial institutions.
  • FACT Morgan Stanley projects private credit will supply an additional $800B in data-center financing over the next two years.

5. Macro and Policy Risk

Financial-Stability Warnings

FACT The IMF's July 2026 World Economic Outlook update flagged AI-related asset risk as reaching "one of the highest [levels] over the last three decades," warning that a correction could cause diminished investment, tighter credit, reduced consumption, and disrupted trade flows.

FACT IMF economist Tobias Adrian specifically stated that leverage, not valuation alone, is the greater concern — elevated debt financing AI investment, combined with rapid capital-obsolescence risk, increases uncertainty around future earnings and asset values.

FACT The IMF also noted the valuation gap between the technology sector and the rest of the S&P 500 is widening faster than regulatory frameworks are adapting.

FACT The Bank for International Settlements' 2026 Annual Report separately named circular AI financing — alongside a potential AI capex bust and sovereign debt fragility — as one of three biggest risks to global financial stability, an independent regulatory voice corroborating the IMF's warning.

Trade and Export-Control Risk: A Separate Axis

VIEW Everything else in this report concerns risks endogenous to how the AI buildout is financed and accounted for. Trade policy is a different category: exogenous, politically driven, and capable of moving on a timeline none of the financial participants control. It belongs in the risk picture precisely because it would not be caught by any amount of balance-sheet analysis.

  • FACT In late August 2026 US authorities opened a probe into Apex Logistics over alleged smuggling of Nvidia AI chips to China — an enforcement action against the logistics layer rather than the chipmaker, but one that signals tightening scrutiny of how restricted hardware moves.
  • FACT Chip tariffs have been publicly flagged by economists as a mechanism that would raise the cost of the AI buildout itself — a direct margin risk to every company in Section 8's "bedrock," which is precisely the layer this report cites as evidence that demand is genuine.
  • FACT On the memory side, Chinese producer CXMT is named in analyst coverage as a competitive threat to Micron's pricing power. Memory is currently the strongest profit pool in the supply chain, so pricing pressure there would erode one of the clearest pieces of independent evidence for real demand.

VIEW Note the asymmetry with the financing risks: export controls and tariffs would hit the bedrock (component makers' margins and volumes), not the leveraged second tier directly. That makes this axis roughly orthogonal to Fault Lines 1–3 — it could deteriorate while the financing structure is perfectly sound, or vice versa.

6. Bull vs. Bear: The Case on Both Sides

Bear Case

  • Circular deal structure obscures true organic demand vs. vendor-financed demand
  • OpenAI's commitments (~$1.1–1.4T) still vastly exceed revenue and near-term visibility, even after the run-rate rose to ~$40B
  • Depreciation schedules extended precisely as chip refresh cycles have shortened (~$176B potential understatement, 2026–2028)
  • Off-balance-sheet lease/SPV debt ($969B undiscounted, $662B not yet commenced) exceeds on-balance-sheet debt at top hyperscalers
  • IMF: highest AI-related asset risk in three decades; leverage is the primary transmission channel
  • CoreWeave and Oracle already show the market repricing sharply on these specific concerns

Bull Case

  • Spenders (Google, Meta, Microsoft, Amazon — ~$725B combined 2026 capex) are among the most profitable companies in history; capex is largely earnings-funded, not equity-dilution-funded
  • Nvidia's ~53% net margin and ~24–26x forward earnings contrast sharply with Cisco's ~472x earnings at the 2000 dot-com peak
  • Unlike dot-com-era pre-revenue startups, today's leaders have real, growing cash flows
  • Older GPUs are holding their economics far better than the bear case assumes — five-year-old A100s still rent at full rates, with CoreWeave capacity contracted to 2029 (Section 2)
  • Technology usefulness may be real even where business ROI lags. The widely-cited "95% of AI pilots show no measurable ROI" MIT finding is real but narrower than its headline: it counted only deployments past pilot stage with measured P&L impact six months on, excluding efficiency, cost and churn gains — so it understates realised value
  • Emerging categories (healthcare diagnostics, scientific discovery) could extend the demand runway beyond chatbot-era assumptions

VIEW Both sides draw on overlapping data and reach opposite conclusions. Whether current spending is "necessary infrastructure" or "bubble excess" is fundamentally a forward-looking judgment call, not a settled fact.

7. Decomposing "Circular Financing": Four Distinct Mechanisms

VIEW "Circular financing" is treated in most commentary as a single phenomenon, which is why the bull/bear debate above tends to talk past itself. In fact it bundles four structurally different mechanisms with materially different risk profiles. Decomposed, the picture is considerably clearer than a blanket "good" or "bad" verdict.

Mechanism 1

Equity Vendor Financing

Risk: Low–Medium

Example: Nvidia → OpenAI, Anthropic, xAI/SpaceX, CoreWeave.

A supplier takes an equity stake in, or extends financing to, a customer to help it afford the supplier's product. This has direct historical precedent (Cisco, dot-com era) and is disclosed, not hidden.

FACT Nvidia's version of this is funded from cash and marketable securities, not debt: $50.3B in cash + marketable securities, $43.4B in non-marketable strategic investments, against total debt of just $8.5B and a 4.3% debt-to-equity ratio. The vendor itself is not levered to make these bets.

Mechanism 2

Related-Party Joint Ventures

Risk: Medium–High

Example: Stargate (SoftBank + OpenAI + Oracle + MGX).

The customer generating the "demand signal" is simultaneously an equity owner of the asset being built. There is no independent third party validating that the capacity is worth what it is priced at — OpenAI is both landlord and sole tenant.

VIEW Fully disclosed, but structurally a related-party transaction rather than an arm's-length sale. The consequence is analytical rather than ethical: it weakens "demand" as an independently verifiable signal, because the buyer and the seller are partly the same party.

Mechanism 3

Leverage in the Undercapitalized Second Tier

Risk: High

Example: OpenAI ($1.1–1.4T commitments vs. a ~$40B revenue run-rate), CoreWeave ($50.8B total liabilities, ongoing losses), Oracle's borrowing to build contracted capacity.

This is the tier that maps onto the dangerous historical precedent: debt concentrated in thinly-capitalized counterparties, not the well-capitalized chip supplier.

FACT CoreWeave and Oracle have already shown sharp, correlated stock drawdowns tied to exactly this concern (Section 4) — this is not a hypothetical risk, it has already repriced equities.

Mechanism 4

Contestable Depreciation Accounting

Risk: Unresolved

Example: Microsoft (4→6yr), Meta (5.5yr) GPU depreciation schedules vs. Burry's ~2–3yr estimate of realistic useful life.

Not circularity in the strict sense, but it compounds the uncertainty: if reported hyperscaler earnings are overstated by extending useful-life assumptions, the "real profitability" bulls cite as reassurance is itself partly unverified.

VIEW Not fully settled, but no longer evenly balanced: secondary-market evidence (five-year-old A100s still renting at full rates, CoreWeave contracting them to 2029) currently runs against Burry's 2–3 year premise. See Section 2.

The Cisco Precedent, With Actual Numbers

FACT In 2000, Cisco's vendor financing was ~10% of its $20B revenue (~$2B). Lucent had committed $8.1B (with $2.1B drawn), Nortel $3.1B ($1.4B drawn), Cisco itself $2.4B. By 2001, Cisco had $1.3B in financed-lease exposure across 735 customers, including $233M to high-risk borrowers, and ultimately wrote off ~$900M in bad loans. The failure mode was not that vendor financing existed — it was that the financed customers (undercapitalized CLECs) collapsed first, and the vendors who had extended them credit absorbed the loss. — MonitorDaily, Technostatecraft

What This Decomposition Establishes — and What It Hands to Section 8

Averaging the four mechanisms into one verdict is where most of the public debate goes wrong. Mechanism 1 (Nvidia's own equity stakes) is disclosed, cash-funded, and has legitimate precedent — calling it inherently bad overstates the case, and Jensen Huang's "the risk is low" claim has real balance-sheet support for Nvidia specifically. Mechanism 2 weakens how reliably "demand" can be read as independent of the financing that produced it. Mechanism 3 is where money is genuinely at risk. Mechanism 4 is contested but, on current evidence, tilting away from the bear case.

This section is a taxonomy of structures: what kinds of circularity exist and how each one works. It deliberately does not deliver an overall risk verdict, because two of the four mechanisms are descriptive rather than dangerous. Section 8 does the second job — establishing what is actually solid underneath all of this, and then carrying forward only the mechanisms that represent live exposure. 8. Synthesis: The Bedrock and the Fault Lines

VIEW Section 7 catalogued the structures. This section does the different job of locating the risk: what is demonstrably solid underneath the whole buildout, and then the four points where it could actually fail. Only two of Section 7's four mechanisms carry live exposure and are carried forward here (per the mapping table that closes that section); the other two fault lines come from outside the circular-financing frame entirely.

VIEW The debate is usually compressed into a single yes/no question: is this a bubble? The more precise framing separates a solid foundation from four genuinely independent points of failure sitting on top of it — independent in the sense that any one could resolve badly without the others following, and vice versa. ("Layer" is deliberately avoided here: in AI infrastructure that word implies a dependent stack, hardware under infrastructure under model under application. These risks aren't stacked that way — they're parallel, not sequential.)

The Bedrock: Demand Is Real, and Broader Than the Named Deals

FACT Recent earnings across the AI infrastructure supply chain show broad-based demand well beyond the handful of circularly-financed names in Section 1. In the most recent reporting quarter: memory makers Micron (+21.4% EPS surprise), SanDisk (+13.7%, EPS trajectory from -$0.93 to $39.25 in roughly 18 months) and Western Digital (+7.9%) are in an accelerating, supply-constrained pricing cycle. Optical/interconnect names Credo (+12.2%, consistent 27–44% beats over the past year), Lumentum (+8.8%, 8 of 8 quarters beat), Arista (+15.2%), and Coherent (+7.6%) show broad, independently-sourced demand for the interconnect hardware that wires GPU clusters together. Cooling/power-equipment maker Vertiv beat cleanly for 8 straight quarters (+6.4% most recently).

VIEW None of these companies is financially entangled with Nvidia, OpenAI, Oracle, or CoreWeave the way those four are with each other. Their independent strength is the strongest evidence available that underlying demand for AI infrastructure is genuine, not manufactured by the deal structures described in Section 1.

Fault Line 1

Concentrated Leverage

High risk, contained to specific names

Debt funding the physical buildout sits almost entirely in two companies — Oracle (-$24.5B free cash flow, $167B debt) and CoreWeave (1,027% debt/equity) — both financing capacity against one customer's future promises. Nvidia and Microsoft, by contrast, are cash-funded and could absorb a slowdown without existential risk (Sections 3–4).

Fault Line 2

Contested Depreciation Accounting

Open, but evidence now leans against the bear case

Even if every circular deal is clean and every counterparty solvent, if Microsoft's and Meta's extended GPU useful-life assumptions (4→6yr, 5.5yr) don't match reality, today's reported profits are overstated regardless (Section 2). But the secondary market is currently the bulls' best evidence anywhere in this report: five-year-old A100s still rent at full rates and CoreWeave has them contracted to 2029. A GAAP judgment call, and one the observable data increasingly supports.

Fault Line 3

Incentive-Alignment Entanglements

Low risk today, worth monitoring

Structures like OpenAI's AMD warrant give counterparties a direct financial stake in each other's stock price, not just the underlying commercial relationship. Not manipulation — vesting requires verifiable physical deployment, not just a stock pop — but a real incentive for both parties to manage the public narrative around the partnership, not just execute it (Section 1).

Fault Line 4

Trade and Export-Control Policy

Exogenous — outside the financing frame

Chip tariffs, export-control enforcement (the Apex Logistics smuggling probe), and Chinese memory competition from CXMT would raise buildout costs and compress component-maker margins (Section 5). Note this one inverts the others: it strikes the bedrock — the independent suppliers whose earnings are the best evidence demand is real — rather than the leveraged second tier. It could deteriorate while the financing structure stays perfectly sound.

VIEW Three things fall out of this, and the second is the most important.

First — the top four rows are straightforward arithmetic on reported figures and can be relied on. Second — the chain breaks at Nvidia. Its customer disclosure is granular enough to establish that concentration exists but too coarse to trace a Microsoft slowdown to a specific revenue figure, so any analysis claiming to quantify that link precisely is estimating, not measuring. That opacity is itself a finding. Third — the bottom row is where a slowdown produces dollars you can actually name, and it lands on the company least able to absorb them: an $800M–$1.4B revenue hit is survivable in isolation, but it would arrive at a business already burning $9.1B of free cash flow a year against $5.5B of cash and $51.6B of debt.

Modelling caveat: the mapping from "Azure growth slows to X%" to "Microsoft cuts CoreWeave spend by Y%" is an illustrative elasticity, not a derived or disclosed relationship — Microsoft could equally absorb a slowdown by trimming owned capex rather than third-party rentals. The row shows the scale of what is exposed, not a forecast of what would happen. And note the whole table stress-tests a chain currently running hot in every link: Azure beat consensus, Nvidia beat and guided up, CoreWeave grew 112% year over year.

Why These Don't Need to Resolve the Same Way

Fault Line 1 could go fine (OpenAI's revenue keeps climbing toward its committed spend, Oracle and CoreWeave grow into their debt) while Fault Line 4 turns bad anyway (tariffs and export controls squeeze the component makers regardless of how sound the financing is) — or the reverse. Fault Line 2 currently looks like it is resolving in the bulls' favour even as Fault Line 1 stays genuinely dangerous. A single "bubble / no bubble" verdict forces four independent bets into one answer the evidence does not support treating as one.

VIEW The single most consequential resolving event sits at the intersection of the bedrock and Fault Line 1: OpenAI's reported run-rate topping $40B ahead of a possible IPO (Section 1) would, if it happens, force public disclosure onto the one counterparty whose payment capacity determines whether Oracle's and CoreWeave's leveraged bets pay off. It would not touch Fault Lines 2 or 4 at all — those resolve on their own timelines regardless of what OpenAI discloses. What to watch, roughly in order of how soon it would be visible: whether CoreWeave's analyst target dispersion (currently $39–$317) narrows or stays wide; whether older-GPU rental rates and residual values hold as new supply arrives, which is the live test of Fault Line 2; movement on chip tariffs or export-control enforcement; the terms of an actual OpenAI IPO filing, if one comes; and, furthest out, whether 2028 hyperscaler capex guidance carries any early GPU replacement or impairment charges.

9. Value-Investing Lens

“It's only when the tide goes out that you learn who has been swimming naked.”
— Warren Buffett, on leverage-driven booms

The circular-financing structure is precisely the kind of arrangement Buffett has historically warned about: revenue and earnings quality that depend on continued participation by a small, interlinked set of counterparties. If any single node in the loop (OpenAI's funding, CoreWeave's liquidity, Oracle's borrowing capacity) falters, the effect propagates through the others — a structural fragility distinct from ordinary cyclical demand risk.

“All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there.”
— Charlie Munger, on avoiding foreseeable failure modes

Munger's inversion framework applied here: the foreseeable failure mode is not "AI doesn't work" but "AI works, yet the capital structure financing it collapses first." Off-balance-sheet debt exceeding on-balance-sheet debt at the largest hyperscalers is exactly the kind of complexity Munger counseled avoiding — not because complexity is inherently bad, but because it obscures the honest accounting an investor needs to assess risk.

“超越自己能力圈的钱，不赚。” (Money outside my circle of competence, I don't earn.)
— 段永平 (Duan Yongping)

Assessing whether a specific hyperscaler's depreciation schedule is defensible requires domain expertise in both semiconductor lifecycle economics and accounting judgment — a genuinely difficult circle-of-competence test. The honest answer for most investors is that the depreciation debate is not fully resolvable from public information alone, which argues for position sizing that respects that uncertainty rather than conviction in either direction.

“能力圈的本质，不是你知道什么，而是你知道你不知道什么。” (The essence of circle of competence is not what you know, but knowing what you don't know.)
— 李录 (Li Lu)

Li Lu's framing is directly applicable to the off-balance-sheet SPV structures: an outside investor cannot fully verify the terms, recourse provisions, or counterparty concentration inside privately negotiated data-center debt facilities. That opacity is itself the risk signal — not proof of wrongdoing, but a limit on how confidently anyone outside the deal can price the downside.

10. Bottom Line

What is established fact

The circular-deal structure exists and is documented across named transactions. OpenAI's committed spend materially exceeds current revenue, though the gap has narrowed since FY2025 as its disclosed run-rate rose to over $40B ahead of a reported IPO. Hyperscaler depreciation schedules have lengthened in the same window GPU refresh cycles have shortened. Off-balance-sheet lease/debt obligations ($969B undiscounted, per Moody's) exceed on-balance-sheet debt at the largest hyperscalers. CoreWeave and Oracle have both had sharp, documented drawdowns tied to these specific concerns, and CoreWeave's ~8x analyst target dispersion shows the market has not converged on how to price the risk. Both the IMF and the BIS have formally flagged this as a financial-stability risk, citing leverage as the primary channel.

What remains open / a matter of interpretation

Whether OpenAI's demand and pricing power will close its funding gap; whether current infrastructure spend will be validated by future AI monetization or written down; and how far trade policy raises the cost of the buildout. On the depreciation question the report does now lean: the secondary market for older GPUs — five-year-old A100s renting at full rates, CoreWeave contracting them to 2029 — runs against Burry's 2–3 year premise, though an easing of the current GPU shortage would be the real test. Elsewhere this report deliberately does not adjudicate; it lays out the data and the competing interpretations so the reader can weigh them.
